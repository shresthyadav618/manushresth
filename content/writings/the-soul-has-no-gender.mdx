There is something strange about the way we speak about one another.

We take something enormous and place it inside something small.

Man.

Woman.

As if two words could contain everything a person is.

But there is always something left outside the definition.

Something that cannot be measured by the body, explained by biology, or predicted by a category.

Call it the soul, if you believe in one.

Call it consciousness.

Call it the self.

Call it simply the strange interior world that every person carries and nobody else can completely see.

Whatever we call it, it feels larger than gender.

---

A person can be a man and still be gentle.

A woman can be strong without becoming hard.

A man can be frightened.

A woman can be angry.

A man can be nurturing.

A woman can be ambitious.

A man can be sensitive.

A woman can be emotionally distant.

None of these things make a person less of what they are.

They simply remind us that human beings were never meant to fit neatly inside the boxes we keep building for them.

We are much stranger than that.

And much more beautiful.

---

I think that is what gets lost when hatred begins to organize itself around gender.

A person stops being a person.

They become a representative.

One bad experience becomes evidence.

One cruel man becomes "men."

One cruel woman becomes "women."

A betrayal becomes a theory.

A disappointment becomes an ideology.

And suddenly millions of strangers are being judged for the actions of people they have never met.

There is something intellectually lazy about that.

But more importantly, there is something morally wrong with it.

Because the individual disappears.

---

There is a difference between recognizing patterns of harm and turning an entire category of people into an enemy.

We should be able to talk honestly about violence, discrimination, abuse, misogyny, misandry, inequality, power, and the ways societies can fail people.

Silence is not kindness.

Pretending that harm doesn't exist is not compassion.

But criticism should have a target.

If someone does something cruel, hold the person responsible.

If an institution produces injustice, challenge the institution.

If a cultural idea causes harm, question the idea.

But hatred of an entire gender asks us to do something intellectually much cheaper.

It asks us to stop looking at individuals.

---

Maybe that is why gender hatred feels so empty to me.

It promises a simple explanation for a complicated world.

It gives pain somewhere to point.

Instead of saying:

*That person hurt me.*

it becomes:

*People like that are all the same.*

The second sentence feels more powerful.

But it is also less true.

And sometimes we mistake the feeling of certainty for the truth itself.

---

Every person we meet is carrying a history we cannot see.

There are fears they have never said out loud.

Memories they still carry.

Things they regret.

Things they are proud of.

People they miss.

Things they are still learning how to forgive.

There are versions of themselves they have outgrown.

There are versions they are still trying to become.

You cannot see any of that from a category.

You have to meet the person.

---

Perhaps that is what makes the human soul beautiful.

Not that we are perfect.

We aren't.

But that we are never completely reducible.

There is always more.

More contradiction.

More possibility.

More history.

More tenderness.

More darkness.

More capacity to change.

A person can disappoint you today and surprise you tomorrow.

Someone can be strong in one part of life and fragile in another.

Someone can carry kindness and selfishness in the same body.

We are not equations.

We are unfinished.

---

And maybe growing up means becoming suspicious of simple hatred.

Not because everyone is good.

They aren't.

Not because everyone deserves trust.

They don't.

And certainly not because we should ignore injustice in the name of being "neutral."

But because hatred is often too blunt an instrument for a world this complicated.

You can have boundaries without contempt.

You can recognize injustice without dehumanizing people.

You can leave someone without hating everyone who resembles them.

You can disagree deeply without turning difference into disgust.

That distinction matters.

---

I don't think the answer is to pretend gender doesn't exist.

It obviously does.

It shapes bodies, experiences, expectations, histories, opportunities, relationships, and the way people move through the world.

But gender is a part of a person.

It is not the entirety of one.

And the moment we forget that, we start speaking about categories as though categories can love, betray, suffer, create, apologize, change, or dream.

They can't.

People can.

---

Maybe that is the thing worth remembering.

Before someone is a man or a woman, they are a person standing in front of you.

A consciousness.

A history.

A collection of memories nobody else possesses.

A future that hasn't happened yet.

Someone who has been loved.

Someone who has been hurt.

Someone who will hurt others.

Someone who will probably be hurt again.

Someone capable of becoming better.

Someone capable of becoming worse.

Someone, ultimately, much more complicated than the word we use to describe their gender.

---

The soul has no category.

It doesn't know about the arguments we have constructed around it.

It doesn't care which side of an internet war someone belongs to.

It simply exists there, quietly, behind the face.

And maybe the most human thing we can do is remember that it is there.

To look at another person and resist the temptation to reduce them.

To see the individual before the category.

The person before the argument.

The human before the gender.

Because hatred may make the world feel simpler.

But understanding asks us to see more.

And seeing more is harder.

It is also, I think,

where our humanity begins.
